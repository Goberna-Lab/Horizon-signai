# Horizon Signai

A web tool that tells a lunar mission planner whether a landing site and date at the Moon's south pole will have sunlight for power and a direct line of sight to Earth for communication, and what to change when the answer is no.

- **Live site:** https://signal-horizon.goberna.us
- **Challenge:** [Lunar Landing Site Navigator](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/2026/challenges/lunar-landing-site-navigator/), NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026, category CLPS
- **Submission text (English):** [docs/SUBMISSION.md](docs/SUBMISSION.md)

## The problem

At the Moon's south pole the Sun never rises more than 1.6° above a level horizon, and Earth sits near the horizon too. A nearby ridge can hide either one for days. A lander needs the Sun for power and Earth for a direct radio link, so the site and the date decide whether the mission works.

The data to check this exists, but in the challenge's words, evaluating each combination "means navigating multiple interfaces and interpreting raw data". Schedules also move: when a launch slips, the planner has to check the site and date again, quickly.

Our user is the mission planner who has to pick a landing site and date, and pick again when the schedule slips, without being an ephemeris or lunar-mapping specialist. Educators and the public are the second audience.

## What it does

| Screen | What you can do there |
|---|---|
| [Landing `/`](https://signal-horizon.goberna.us/) | Scroll through a flight over the lunar south pole. It explains the low Sun, power, the Earth link, and why two points a few hundred metres apart on the same ridge can have very different sunlight. Every figure on the page is generated by the validated engine. |
| [Planner `/planner#landing`](https://signal-horizon.goberna.us/planner#landing) | Opens on a schedule slip: the example plan lands on 18 December 2026, and the opening scenario moves it to 3 April 2027 with the same equipment and requirements. Buttons move the landing by a day or a week, and one click goes back to the plan. Pick a candidate zone, set the scenario (landing date and time in UTC, length of stay, solar panel height, antenna height, battery hours, minimum Earth contact) and get a **GO / REVIEW / NO GO** verdict against four requirements. If the plan fails, the tool proposes how to comply: another date, another zone, or taller equipment. Compare up to 6 saved scenarios. Add your own point within 190 km of the pole. See the Sun and Earth drawn against the real terrain horizon with the time left until each one sets, a timeline from 30 days before to 60 days after landing, and a descent simulation. The **Link** button copies an address that reopens the same zone and scenario, to share it with the science or engineering team. |
| [Missions `/planner`](https://signal-horizon.goberna.us/planner) | An example campaign around the planner: next milestone, decisions waiting for sign-off, a campaign calendar and a log. The missions, sign-offs and log are sample data; the sites and the Sun and Earth figures are real. |
| [Mission workspace `/dashboard`](https://signal-horizon.goberna.us/dashboard) | Follow several missions on a LOLA map of the south pole. An alert appears when a schedule change makes a mission fail. Each mission has a stay timeline that exports to CSV. |
| [3D Moon `/3d-map`](https://signal-horizon.goberna.us/3d-map) | Explore a globe with LOLA relief and a detailed south pole. 62 landing sites (Apollo, Surveyor, Luna, Chang'e, Chandrayaan, CLPS deliveries, south pole candidate regions and more), each with its source. 8,876 IAU place names. Pick a date between 2026 and 2030 to see the real Sun and Earth, and each site's Sun and Earth elevation against its horizon. Any point within 190 km of the south pole opens in the planner with one click, on the date shown. |
| EVA (on every screen) | A small 3D guide robot that explains each screen and answers questions in English or Spanish, with a synthesized voice. She explains figures computed by the engine; she never computes them herself. |

**The four requirements** behind the verdict: the Sun is in view at landing; Earth is in view at landing; the longest night during the stay is no longer than the battery lasts; Earth is in view for at least the chosen share of the stay. REVIEW means the plan passes, but the Sun or Earth clears the terrain by less than 0.5° at landing.

**Planner zones** are regions of interest published with NASA's 5 m south pole terrain models (Barker et al. 2021): Connecting Ridge RoI 6, Peak near Shackleton RoI 2 and Shackleton rim RoI 4.

**Example of a schedule slip** (planner): with a 3-day stay, panel and antenna at 2 m, a 24 h battery and 40% minimum Earth contact, all three zones are GO for a landing on 18 December 2026. Move the landing to 3 April 2027 and zones A and C become NO GO (the Sun is hidden at landing and the night is longer than the battery), while zone B is still GO. Two days later zone A is GO again. The plan is an example; the figures are computed.

**Example of a schedule slip** (workspace): the example mission INTI-2 moved from 8 to 29 March 2027. On the new date Earth is hidden at landing and the longest night is 105 h against a 50 h battery, so the mission fails. The tool proposes 6 April 2027. The example missions, their dates and their requirements are invented to show the tool; the sites are real and the figures are computed.

## How it is validated

Each check compares the engine with an external source or an independent calculation, not with itself.

- **Sun and Earth directions.** The planner uses one Moon-fixed table for the whole Moon. JPL Horizons, queried directly from two sites, is an independent calculation. Over 4,266 hourly rows at Apollo 17, 4,267 at Connecting Ridge and 12,137 rows of an 18.6-year series, the worst difference is 0.0065°. The test requires 0.01° or less. The remainder is annual aberration, which is not applied.
- **Sunlight and Earth visibility at the pole.** At the centre of Connecting Ridge, at ground level over 18.6 years, the engine gives the Sun in view 36.6% of the time and Earth 30.3%. The published maps of Mazarico et al. (2011) give 38.5% and 33.9%. The test allows ±5 points. Across 26 catalogue points, the mean Earth error is 4.3 points.
- **Candidate regions.** Barker et al. (2021) report that regions 4 to 6 of Connecting Ridge exceed 70% illumination at 1 m and are the best of the site. The engine, at 1 m over 2026 to 2030, gives 88.0%, 85.0% and 90.3%, above the other regions of the site.
- **Two independent paths inside the project.** The planner (global table plus horizon mask) and the earlier 3D scene engine (per-site tables plus ray marching over the terrain model) agree within ±0.5 points at Apollo 17 and Connecting Ridge, at three observer heights.
- **Approximate horizons.** The 240 m horizon used for custom points differs from the detailed one by 0.20° RMS from a 10 m mast at Connecting Ridge. See the limits below for ground level.
- **Descent.** The simulated descent is compared with the Apollo 11 pre-mission profile in NASA TN D-6846: touchdown within 5 s of the report's 714 s, with velocity change and propellant about 1% low. One known miss is recorded in the tests: throttle recovery comes 24 s late, outside the ±15 s tolerance.
- **Automated tests.** The engine's original suite passed 113 tests with 0 failures on 14 November 2026. The copy used by the app has its own tests (`npm test`), which include a recorded example: Connecting Ridge, lunar day from 1 December 2026, Sun 56.8%, Earth 19.9%.
- **3D map.** A script (`apps/web/scripts/mapa-3d/verificar-mapa.py`) checks the map against NSSDCA, PGDA, the IAU Gazetteer, the LOLA files and JPL Horizons.

Details, tolerances and what is not covered: [docs/07-pruebas-y-validacion.md](docs/07-pruebas-y-validacion.md) and [prototipos/lunar-map/README.md](prototipos/lunar-map/README.md).

## Assumptions and limits

- **Scope.** The tool evaluates sunlight and Earth visibility. It does not assess slopes, hazards or any other landing risk, and it does not certify a site.
- **Dates.** 2026 to 2030 only. Times are UTC.
- **Horizon.** The Moon has no atmosphere, so there is no refraction: the horizon is set by terrain and the Moon's curvature.
- **Sun and Earth.** The Sun counts as visible when any part of its disc is above the horizon. Earth is counted by its centre.
- **Power.** The solar panel is assumed vertical and turning to face the Sun. Irradiance is 1361 W/m² times the visible fraction of the disc times the cosine of the Sun's elevation. This is irradiance on the panel, not the output of a specific power system.
- **Equipment height.** The Sun is measured from the panel height and Earth from the antenna height, at the same ground point.
- **Custom points.** Only within 190 km of the south pole. Their horizon comes from the 240 m LOLA grid and is flagged as approximate on screen. At ground level on ridges and crater rims it can differ from the 5 m model by up to about 40 points of sunlight. Decide with a catalogue zone.
- **Requirements.** The thresholds are set by the user. The defaults (for example a 24 h battery and 40% Earth contact in the planner) are examples, not values from a real mission.
- **Descent simulation.** It flies the Apollo 11 profile and propellant as a validated example vehicle. It is not a CLPS lander and does not depend on the site.
- **Illustrative maps.** The maps inside `/planner` and its surface-walk step use generated relief for illustration, not the real terrain model. The verdict and figures do not come from those maps.
- **3D map.** Approximate items are marked on screen: sites with approximate coordinates (NASA does not publish a centre for each Artemis III region), 240 m horizons, and slopes that depend on scale.
- **Testing.** The engine and data are tested automatically. The interface has been reviewed by hand, not tested with users.

## Run it locally

Requires Node 22.

```sh
npm ci
npm run dev        # web (Vite, http://localhost:5173) and API (Hono, http://localhost:3000)
npm test           # unit tests
npx tsc --noEmit -p apps/api && npm run build -w @horizon-signai/web   # the same checks CI runs
```

The landing, `/planner` and `/dashboard` work with these commands. `/planner` is served by the API, so the API must be running.

`/3d-map` reads its data from PostgreSQL + PostGIS. Put `POSTGRES_DB`, `POSTGRES_USER`, `POSTGRES_PASSWORD` and `DATABASE_URL` in a `.env` file at the repository root, then:

```sh
docker compose -f db/docker-compose.yml up -d    # PostGIS on host port 5433
pip install -r db/requirements.txt
python db/migrate.py
python db/etl/load_map3d.py
# then in psql: CALL moon.refresh_site_illumination();
python db/etl/verify.py
```

The API needs `DATABASE_URL` in its environment. Without it the API still starts and `/api/map3d` answers 503. Details: [db/README.md](db/README.md).

EVA's answers and voice come from model servers on the team's private network, so they are not available in a local copy. Everything else works without them.

Production runs with `docker-compose.prod.yml` (database, data loader and app) and deploys from `main` through GitHub Actions.

## Use of AI

- **Development.** The code and the documentation, including this README, were developed with an AI coding assistant (Claude Code, by Anthropic). Most commits carry a `Co-Authored-By` line naming the assistant.
- **EVA.** Her answers are generated by a language model and her voice is synthesized. Both run on the team's own servers with open models. The defaults in the code are Qwen models served by Ollama (`qwen3.6:27b` for answers, `qwen3:8b` to classify interruptions in voice conversation), Kokoro-82M for speech and faster-whisper `large-v3-turbo` for speech recognition.
- **Rule.** The language model never computes a figure. The engine computes; the model explains. Generated answers can still be wrong: the numbers on screen are the reference.
- **Review.** In the earlier prototype, an independent AI agent reviewed the interface design against a design reference book. This is recorded in `docs/07-pruebas-y-validacion.md`.
